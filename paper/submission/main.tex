\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Background.} Federated brain tumour classifiers on public MRI collections report accuracies above
92\%, yet the merged sources contain duplicated images, and privacy claims rarely state an accounted budget or a
privacy unit.

\noindent\textbf{Purpose.} This study measures what formally accounted differential privacy (DP) costs and protects
in federated brain tumour MRI classification, at the level of single images and of whole patients, on a deduplicated
benchmark.

\noindent\textbf{Methods.} Figshare, SARTAJ and Br35H images were deduplicated by perceptual hashing into 3845 images
with patient-disjoint splits, and a later correlation check removed 66 mirrored near-duplicates from the test set. A
pretrained ResNet-18 was trained centrally and with FedAvg, FedProx and SCAFFOLD under record-level and
patient-level DP-SGD, with R\'enyi accounting at $\delta=10^{-5}$ and five seeds per setting. Leakage was audited
with LiRA using 16 shadow models, and Grad-CAM was scored against expert tumour masks and across seeds.

\noindent\textbf{Results.} On 747 clean test images, central balanced accuracy fell from 0.908 without DP to 0.778
at record-level $\varepsilon=8$, while patient-level DP needed $\varepsilon=64$ to reach 0.820. Under DP, accuracy
declined with client count at fixed data, by 0.096 per unit of $\ln K$ at $\varepsilon=3$. For federated models
without DP, LiRA identified 10.8\% of training images at 1\% false positives and training patients with AUC 0.805,
whereas every DP setting stayed at chance. Unclipped weight noise at matched utility still leaked, and Grad-CAM
consistency fell from 0.70 to 0.24 in step with accuracy.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions.} Record-level DP at $\varepsilon=8$ removes measurable image and patient leakage at a
cost of 0.08 (federated) to 0.13 (central) in balanced accuracy, whereas a formal per-patient guarantee costs far
more at this data scale.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Federated learning lets hospitals train a shared tumour classifier without pooling scans, and early brain tumour
studies showed that federated models approach the accuracy of centrally trained ones.\cite{sheller2019,sheller2020,pati2022}
A large body of later work evaluates the same idea on public four-class MRI collections assembled from Figshare,
SARTAJ and Br35H, with reported accuracies between 92\% and 99\%.\cite{albalawi2024,mastoi2025,muntaqim2025,paul2026}

Those collections overlap. Vangala et al.\ found that 28.8\% of a widely used Kaggle test split has a near-twin in
training and that most traceable Figshare test images share a patient with training images.\cite{vangala2026}
Accuracy measured on such splits partly rewards memorised duplicates, so it cannot show what privacy protection
costs a model that must generalise to unseen patients.

Privacy claims in the same literature are seldom checkable. Several federated studies add noise but do not state
the privacy budget $\varepsilon$, the failure probability $\delta$ or the unit being protected.\cite{lakshmi2026,qazi2026}
Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) bounds each example's influence by clipping its gradient
before adding calibrated noise.\cite{abadi2016} The closest prior work on federated brain tumour MRI instead adds
Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.5 to client weights without clipping, so its stated $\varepsilon$ of about
3.4 cannot be derived from the mechanism.\cite{gupta2026}

Formal privacy in medical imaging has a measurable price. Kaissis et al.\ trained a three-site federated model on
paediatric chest radiographs at $\varepsilon=6$, and accuracy fell while remaining comparable to two
radiologists.\cite{kaissis2021} Large budgets can already block reconstruction attacks at small cost.\cite{ziller2024}
Knolle et al.\ showed, however, that patients contributing several similar records can face near-perfect membership
inference even when an aggregate attack looks random.\cite{knolle2026}

That finding matters for slice-based MRI, where one Figshare patient contributes about 13 slices. A guarantee per
image then protects a patient only through group privacy, roughly 13 times weaker, so a privacy unit of one patient
is the clinically meaningful choice. Explanations raise a second question, since DP is known to degrade
gradient-based attributions,\cite{saifullah2024,manna2024} yet localisation against expert tumour masks under
federated DP has not been measured.

This study addresses these gaps on a deduplicated three-source benchmark with expert tumour masks.\cite{cheng2015} It
asks what record-level and patient-level DP cost in accuracy, how client count and drift correction change that cost,
whether measured leakage follows the formal guarantee, and how explanations change under privacy.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A deduplicated, patient-disjoint benchmark.} Perceptual-hash deduplication reduced 10,189 source
files to 3845 images split by patient, and an independent correlation check found 66 mirrored near-duplicates in the
test set, which are excluded from every reported result.
\item \textbf{An accounted privacy-utility map at two privacy units.} Central and federated models were trained under
record-level and patient-level DP-SGD with R\'enyi accounting and five seeds per setting. Record-level
$\varepsilon=8$ cost 0.13 balanced accuracy centrally, while patient-level DP needed $\varepsilon=64$ for the same
utility.
\item \textbf{The noise-scale explanation of client count under DP.} Splitting the same data across more clients
reduced accuracy under DP, by 0.096 per unit of $\ln K$ at $\varepsilon=3$, and the aggregated noise scale predicted
this decline. FedProx and SCAFFOLD did not recover the loss.
\item \textbf{An empirical privacy audit.} LiRA with 16 shadow models per setting detected training images and
patients without DP and found nothing above chance under any DP setting. An unclipped weight-noise mechanism at
matched utility still leaked.
\item \textbf{Explanation fidelity under privacy.} Grad-CAM localisation against expert masks and its consistency
across seeds both fell as $\varepsilon$ shrank, and the fall tracked accuracy closely ($r=0.80$).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the data, training and audit protocol that produced these results.

\section{Material and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data sources and deduplication}
Three public collections were combined, namely Figshare (3064 T1-weighted contrast-enhanced slices from 233 patients
with expert tumour masks),\cite{cheng2015} SARTAJ (3264 images) and Br35H (3861 images). Glioma, meningioma,
pituitary tumour and no-tumour labels were kept, which left 7828 images before deduplication.

Each image was rescaled to 8-bit intensity between its 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles, padded to a square and resized
to $128\times128$ pixels with box filtering. A 64-bit discrete cosine transform perceptual hash was computed per
image. Pairs within Hamming distance 4 were treated as duplicates, and pairs within distance 6, together with all
slices of one Figshare patient, were linked into one split unit.

Duplicate groups kept one image, preferring Figshare over SARTAJ over Br35H. Groups with conflicting labels were
removed (26 images within groups and 23 non-Figshare images conflicting with Figshare), and 48 pairs of Figshare
patients sharing a slice were merged into single units. The curated set holds 3845 images in 821 training and test
units plus 103 validation units (Table~\ref{tab:data}).

Units were assigned to training, validation and test splits with seed 2026, giving 2610, 422 and 813 images with no
Figshare patient in two splits. As a later check, every pair of curated images was compared by normalised
cross-correlation (NCC) of $64\times64$ versions, both as stored and with one image mirrored.

A test image with NCC of at least 0.95 to any image in another split, a threshold fixed before the check, was marked
contaminated. This marked 66 test images, of which 120 of the underlying 148 cross-split pairs matched only after
mirroring, a case the perceptual hash cannot detect. All results use the remaining 747 clean test images, and
full-set values are given in the supplement.

\subsection{Federated partitions}
The natural partition (natural3) assigns each source to one client, giving clients of 2029 (Figshare), 351 (SARTAJ)
and 230 (Br35H) training images with strong label skew, since no-tumour images come only from SARTAJ and Br35H.
Synthetic partitions split the same 2610 images into 10 clients, either uniformly at random or by a Dirichlet prior
over class proportions with $\alpha$ of 0.1, 0.5 or 5.0.\cite{hsu2019}

To test the effect of client count at fixed data, the $\alpha=0.5$ partition was also built with $K$ of 3, 5 and 15
clients. For patient-level DP, a Dirichlet partition over whole units ($\alpha=0.5$, $K=10$) keeps every patient on
one client.

\subsection{Model and training}
All models are an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18\cite{he2016} whose first convolution sums the RGB filters into one
input channel. Batch normalisation layers stay in inference mode with their pretrained statistics, so the network is
a per-example function and per-example clipping remains valid, while their affine parameters are trained.

Federated training uses FedAvg\cite{mcmahan2017} with full participation and size-weighted averaging over 100 rounds.
Each round, every client runs one local epoch of 10 steps with Poisson sampling at rate $q=0.1$, stochastic gradient
descent with momentum 0.9, a cosine learning-rate schedule and random horizontal flips.

Central training is the one-client case on the pooled training set. The released model is the round with the lowest
validation loss. Without DP, gradients are clipped to a global norm of 1.0 for stability.

\subsection{Record-level and patient-level differential privacy}
Record-level DP-SGD\cite{abadi2016} clips each example's gradient $g_i$ to norm $C=1$, adds Gaussian noise to the sum
and divides by the expected batch size $q\,n_j$ of client $j$, as in Eq.~(\ref{eq:dpsgd}), where $B$ is the sampled
batch and $\sigma$ the noise multiplier.
\begin{equation}
\tilde{g} = \frac{1}{q\, n_j}\left( \sum_{i \in B} g_i \min\left(1, \frac{C}{\lVert g_i \rVert_2}\right)
+ \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I\right) \right)
\label{eq:dpsgd}
\end{equation}

The noise multiplier $\sigma$ was calibrated by bisection so that R\'enyi DP accounting\cite{mironov2017} in
Opacus\cite{yousefpour2021} gives the target $\varepsilon$ at $\delta=10^{-5}$ over the 1000 local steps. Targets
were $\varepsilon$ of 1, 3 and 8, plus a clip-only ablation with $\sigma=0$ that separates the effect of clipping from
that of noise.

Patient-level DP replaces images by units. Units are Poisson-sampled at rate $q$, the mean gradient of each sampled
unit is clipped to $C$, and noise is added to the sum before division by $q\,U_j$, where $U_j$ counts the client's
units.

The guarantee then holds per patient, with a unit split across $m$ clients composed over $m$ mechanisms. Central
patient-level models used $\varepsilon$ of 3, 8, 16, 32 and 64, and federated ones $\varepsilon=8$. Because learning
rates were chosen on validation data among $k=2$ candidates trained on the same records, the $\varepsilon$ composed
over both candidates is also reported, following Papernot and Steinke.\cite{papernot2022}

\subsection{Drift correction and the weight-noise comparator}
FedProx\cite{li2020} adds a proximal term with weight $\mu$ to each local objective, and $\mu$ from 0.01 to 10 was
screened on validation loss at seed 0. SCAFFOLD\cite{karimireddy2020} uses option II control variates, with two
corrections required by this setting.

The normaliser of the control variate uses the displacement of heavy-ball momentum, $\eta\,S(K,\beta)$ with
$S=\sum_{k=1}^{K}(1-\beta^k)/(1-\beta)$, instead of $K\eta$, where $\eta$ is the learning rate, $K$ the number of
local steps and $\beta$ the momentum. The server control variate is averaged with the same size weights as the model,
and an ablation keeps the uniform average.

To test the mechanism of the closest prior work,\cite{gupta2026} Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0,s^2)$ was added to each
client's weights after local training, without clipping or accounting. The literal $s=0.5$ was kept, and a
utility-matched $s$ was chosen by a rule fixed beforehand.

That rule took the largest $s$ in $\{0.5, 0.05, 0.01, 0.003, 0.001\}$ whose seed-0 validation loss did not exceed
that of record-level $\varepsilon=8$ FedAvg on natural3, which selected $s=0.003$.

\subsection{Membership inference audit}
The likelihood-ratio attack LiRA\cite{carlini2022} was run against natural3 FedAvg at no DP and at $\varepsilon$ of
8, 3 and 1, and against the weight-noise comparator. Each target configuration has 16 shadow models trained with the
target's own settings and noise multiplier.

Each shadow model trains on a random half of the training and test units, so every patient is wholly in or out. The
attack statistic is the logit of the true-class confidence on the image and its mirror, and online LiRA compares
Gaussian fits to IN and OUT shadow statistics with one pooled variance per side.

Members are the training images and non-members the clean test images. Patient-level scores sum image scores over a
Figshare patient, comparing 126 training with 40 test patients. True-positive rates at 1\% and 0.1\% false-positive
rate are the primary measures, with AUC reported alongside.

\subsection{Explanation metrics}
Grad-CAM\cite{selvaraju2017} on the last residual block, Grad-CAM++\cite{chattopadhay2018} and Integrated
Gradients\cite{sundararajan2017} with a black baseline were computed for the 645 Figshare test tumour images that have
expert masks. Localisation used the pointing game with a 3-pixel tolerance, the IoU of the top 15\% of pixels with the
mask and the share of saliency inside the mask.

Faithfulness used deletion and insertion curves over 20 steps.\cite{petsiuk2018} A model-parameter randomisation
test\cite{adebayo2018} checked that maps depend on learned weights, and the outer 8-pixel rim of the brain was scored
to detect skull-border artefacts.

Consistency across seeds followed Gupta et al.,\cite{gupta2026} with the five seeds of one configuration in place of
clients. It is the IoU between the top 10\% of each model's Grad-CAM pixels and those of the mean map, computed per
image at $8\times8$ resolution.

\subsection{Source controls}
Because no-tumour images come only from SARTAJ and Br35H, accuracy was also computed on SARTAJ alone, the only source
with all four classes. A logistic-regression probe on penultimate features tested whether the source collection is
decodable among images of one class, and the same probe on an ImageNet-initialised and a randomly initialised network
gave baselines.

\subsection{Statistics and reproducibility}
Every setting was trained with seeds 1 to 5, with seed 0 reserved for learning-rate and $\mu$ selection. Results are
seed means with $t$-based 95\% confidence intervals, and SCAFFOLD was compared with FedAvg by paired $t$-tests over
seeds with Holm correction across 15 configurations.

The client-count effect was estimated as the slope of balanced accuracy on $\ln K$ over 20 runs per privacy level.
Explanation metrics within one model carry 95\% intervals from 2000 bootstrap resamples of test patients.

All training ran on Kaggle with two NVIDIA T4 GPUs, about 64 GPU hours in total, from tagged code versions. Each run
is identified by a hash of its resolved configuration. The tables and figures were regenerated on Kaggle from the
stored run files and frozen with SHA-256 hashes before writing.

These procedures produced the measurements reported next, beginning with the accuracy cost of each privacy setting.

Patient-level DP was far more expensive at the same $\varepsilon$. Patient-level clip-only training reached 0.872,
so weighting each patient once did not itself harm accuracy, but at $\varepsilon=8$ accuracy fell to 0.337.

The patient-level curve recovered only at large budgets, with 0.565 at $\varepsilon=16$, 0.727 at $\varepsilon=32$
and 0.820 at $\varepsilon=64$. Matching record-level $\varepsilon=8$ therefore needed a patient-level budget about
eight times larger.

The gap follows from the noise scale. Patient-level noise is divided by the number of units, 634 in training, rather
than by 2610 images, so the same $\sigma$ produces about four times more noise in each averaged update.

Record-level $\varepsilon=8$ is not a weak guarantee per patient by accident. A Figshare patient contributes a median
of 13 slices, so group privacy bounds that patient's loss only at roughly 13 times the per-image budget.

\subsection{Client count under DP}
With the same 2610 images split across more clients, accuracy fell under DP but not without it
(Fig.~\ref{fig:clients}). At $\varepsilon=3$ the slope was $-0.096$ per unit of $\ln K$ ($p<0.0001$), from 0.677 with
three clients to 0.525 with 15.

At $\varepsilon=8$ the decline reached $-0.156$ per unit of $\ln K$ ($p<0.0001$) with a learning rate of 0.01, but
almost vanished with the rate of 0.003 tuned for 10 clients ($-0.016$, $p=0.38$). Without DP the slope was $+0.013$
($p=0.08$).

With size-weighted averaging, the noise in the averaged update has standard deviation $\sigma C\sqrt{K}/(qN)$ whatever
the client sizes, so both privacy levels lie on one curve against this quantity. Client count, not label skew, set the
cost of DP here, which also explains why the three-client natural3 partition held up best at $\varepsilon=1$.

\subsection{Drift correction}
Neither FedProx nor SCAFFOLD recovered the utility lost to DP. FedProx with $\mu$ from 0.01 to 1 changed validation
loss by less than one seed standard deviation in all 15 configurations, and $\mu=10$ made models worse without DP and
at $\varepsilon=8$.

Size-weighted SCAFFOLD did not differ from FedAvg in any configuration after Holm correction, with the smallest
adjusted $p=0.064$. With the uniform server average of the original algorithm, SCAFFOLD collapsed on unequal clients,
falling to 0.590 against 0.825 on natural3 at $\varepsilon=8$ on the full test set.

(Table~\ref{tab:lira}, Fig.~\ref{fig:lira}). At a 1\% false-positive rate it found 10.8\% of training images, and

Under every DP setting the true-positive rate at 1\% false positives stayed between 0.010 and 0.017, the level of a
random guess. Average-case AUC under DP moved slightly away from 0.5 in both directions, which reflects the smaller
training sets of the shadow models rather than leakage, since the low-false-positive tail stayed at chance.

The weight-noise comparator behaved differently. At $s=0.5$, the value of the prior work, training diverged in all 21
runs and produced no usable model. At $s=0.003$ accuracy was 0.801, above FedAvg at $\varepsilon=8$, yet the attack

A simpler loss-threshold attack was weak even without DP, with AUC 0.52 to 0.54. Computed within each source and
class, it stayed below 0.5 under DP, which confirms that its sub-chance values were not caused by the mix of sources
in each split.

\subsection{Explanations}
Grad-CAM localisation against expert masks declined as $\varepsilon$ shrank. For correctly classified test images of
central models, the pointing-game hit rate fell from 0.197 without DP to 0.156 at $\varepsilon=8$ and 0.089 at
$\varepsilon=1$, and the top-15\% IoU fell from 0.087 to 0.032.

Masks cover 1.8\% of the image on average, so these values remain well above chance but far from precise
localisation. Grad-CAM placed 0.17 to 0.19 of its mass on the outer brain rim, below the rim's area share of 0.33, and
placed less mass outside the brain than the area share of the background.

Consistency across seeds fell from 0.700 without DP to 0.663 with clipping alone and to 0.485, 0.309 and 0.235 at
$\varepsilon$ of 8, 3 and 1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:xai}a). Patient-level models stayed between 0.22 and 0.29 up to
$\varepsilon=32$ and reached 0.368 at $\varepsilon=64$.

Across all 115 explained models, consistency correlated with clean balanced accuracy at $r=0.80$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:xai}b), and top-15\% IoU correlated with accuracy at $r=0.85$. Once accuracy entered a regression, the
noise multiplier added no explanatory power for IoU ($p=0.51$) or consistency ($p=0.15$).

Integrated Gradients failed the randomisation test, since maps from a fully re-initialised network kept a Spearman
correlation of 0.68 to 0.71 with the trained maps. Grad-CAM passed, with correlations between $-0.05$ and 0.39, so
its numbers are the ones interpreted here.

\subsection{Source controls}
The source collection was linearly decodable from penultimate features in every setting. Among tumour images, a probe
separated Figshare from SARTAJ with balanced accuracy 0.913 without DP and 0.851 at $\varepsilon=1$, close to the
0.871 of the ImageNet initialisation and the 0.846 of a random network.

Source therefore lives in the images themselves, and training added only about 0.04 to it. Accuracy on SARTAJ alone,
the one source with all four classes, was 0.663 for central models without DP on the full test set, far below the
pooled 0.919, which shows how strongly per-source composition shapes headline accuracy.

These measurements raise questions about why privacy cost so much more per patient than per image, and what the
audit does and does not show, which the next section discusses.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Protection bought by the privacy budget}
The audit and the accounting tell one consistent story. Without DP, a federated model trained on 2610 images leaks
enough for LiRA to flag one training image in ten at a 1\% false-positive rate, and patient-level aggregation makes
the leak clearer still.

Record-level DP at $\varepsilon=8$ removed every measurable signal, at a cost of 0.08 in balanced accuracy for
natural3 FedAvg and 0.13 centrally. This agrees with the view that moderate budgets already defeat practical
attacks,\cite{ziller2024} although an attack failing does not prove that no stronger attack exists.

The weight-noise comparator separates noise from guarantee. Noise added to weights without clipping can be tuned to
keep accuracy, as at $s=0.003$, but it then leaves training patients identifiable with AUC 0.745. At the published
$s=0.5$ it destroys the model, so neither setting supports a stated $\varepsilon$ for that mechanism.\cite{gupta2026}

\subsection{Higher cost of patient-level protection}
Patient-level DP protects the clinically meaningful unit but needs a budget about eight times larger for the same
accuracy. The arithmetic is simple, since the noise in each update is divided by 634 units instead of 2610 images,
and the clip-only control confirms that noise, not per-patient weighting, causes the loss.

This reinforces the finding of Knolle et al.\ that multi-record patients are the real privacy risk.\cite{knolle2026}
At the scale of public brain tumour collections, a strong per-patient guarantee and a useful classifier are not yet
compatible, and a larger number of patients is the most direct remedy.

\subsection{Client count, not heterogeneity}
Under DP, accuracy fell as the same data were split across more clients, while label skew mattered less. The
aggregated noise standard deviation $\sigma C\sqrt{K}/(qN)$ grows with the square root of client count, so each added
client adds independent noise without adding data.

This mechanism is standard DP-SGD arithmetic and is not claimed as new. Its practical consequence is that cross-silo
consortia with few large sites suffer least, and that learning rates tuned for one client count can hide the effect
at another.

Drift correction did not help because one local epoch per round produced little drift, while noise dominated the
error. The SCAFFOLD ablation also shows that control variates must be averaged with the same weights as the model, or
the correction harms exactly the unequal, noisy settings where it is most needed.

\subsection{Explanations under privacy}
DP degraded Grad-CAM localisation and cross-seed consistency, in line with reports that DP harms gradient-based
attributions in general.\cite{saifullah2024,manna2024} The present results add that, for brain tumour MRI, the
degradation follows accuracy closely and the noise level adds nothing once accuracy is known.

For clinical use this means a private model's explanations are about as trustworthy as its predictions, not less. The
clip-only control shows that clipping alone barely changes consistency (0.700 to 0.663), so noise drives the change
through the accuracy it costs.

The low absolute localisation, with a pointing hit rate near 0.2 even without DP, reflects the $4\times4$ resolution
of the last ResNet block at 128-pixel input. Integrated Gradients with a black baseline failed the randomisation test,
which argues against reporting such maps without that check.\cite{adebayo2018}

\subsection{Benchmark leakage and source confounding}
The correlation check found mirrored duplicates that perceptual hashing had missed, concentrated in the no-tumour
class of Br35H. Training with random flips can then show a model the exact test image, and near-perfect no-tumour
specificity on Br35H partly reflected this.

Source is also decodable from images of a single class even by a random network. Headline accuracy on merged public
collections therefore partly measures which collection an image came from, as Vangala et al.\ reported for
centralised benchmarks.\cite{vangala2026}

Both findings suggest that the 92\% to 99\% accuracies reported on these
collections\cite{albalawi2024,mastoi2025,muntaqim2025,paul2026} should not be compared with clinical performance.
Deduplication by a flip-invariant measure and per-source reporting should become routine for these data.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Remaining duplicates in development data.} Mirrored pairs remain between training and validation (47
pairs), so round selection used slightly contaminated validation data, while all test results exclude contaminated
images.
\item \textbf{Look-alike units.} SARTAJ and Br35H carry no patient identifiers, so their units are groups of similar
images, and the patient-level guarantee is exact only for Figshare.
\item \textbf{Shadow-target mismatch.} Shadow models trained on about 1700 images against 2610 for the targets, which
shifts average-case AUC, so conclusions rest on low-false-positive rates.
\item \textbf{Small patient-level test set.} Only 40 Figshare test patients were available, so patient-level
true-positive rates below 2.5\% false positives cannot be resolved.
\item \textbf{Scope.} All models are 2D ResNet-18 classifiers on axial slices from three public sources, and the
federated partitions are simulated rather than real institutions.
\item \textbf{Selection cost.} Learning rates were chosen on private validation data, and the $\varepsilon$ composed
over both candidates is reported but was not used for calibration.
\end{enumerate}

Within these limits, the results support a small number of firm conclusions, stated next.

\section{Conclusions}
On a deduplicated three-source brain MRI benchmark, record-level DP at $\varepsilon=8$ removed all leakage that a
strong membership attack could measure, at a cost of 0.08 to 0.13 in balanced accuracy. Without DP, the same attack
identified training images and, more clearly, training patients.

A formal guarantee per patient cost far more, needing $\varepsilon=64$ to match record-level $\varepsilon=8$, because
noise is shared among patients rather than slices. Under DP, splitting data across more clients lowered accuracy
through the aggregated noise scale, and drift correction did not help.

Weight noise without clipping either destroyed the model or left patients identifiable, so it should not be reported
with an $\varepsilon$. Grad-CAM localisation and consistency degraded with privacy in step with accuracy.

Future federated studies on these public collections should use flip-invariant deduplication, state $\varepsilon$,
$\delta$ and the privacy unit, and test their privacy claims with an attack.
