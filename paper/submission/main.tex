\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Background.} Federated brain tumour classifiers for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on public
collections report accuracies of 94\% and above. These collections overlap, and privacy claims rarely state
the budget or protected unit.

\noindent\textbf{Purpose.} The study measures what accounted differential privacy (DP) costs and protects per
image and patient, and how it alters explanations.

\noindent\textbf{Methods.} Figshare, SARTAJ and Br35H were deduplicated into 3845 images with
patient-disjoint splits, and a mirror-aware check removed 66 contaminated test images. A pretrained ResNet-18 was
trained centrally and with federated averaging (FedAvg) under record- and patient-level DP stochastic gradient descent
(R\'enyi accounting, $\delta=10^{-5}$, five seeds). A likelihood-ratio attack (LiRA, 16 shadow models) with
source- and class-matched non-members audited leakage, and gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) was scored against expert masks.

\noindent\textbf{Results.} Clean-set central balanced accuracy fell from 0.908 without DP to 0.778
at record-level $\varepsilon=8$, and patient-level DP needed $\varepsilon=64$ to reach 0.820. Against non-private
FedAvg, LiRA found 11.1\% of training images at a 1\% false-positive rate and separated training from test patients
with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.805, whereas under record-level DP it found 1.0\% to 1.5\%, not significantly
above chance. Higher-utility unclipped weight noise still exposed patients (AUC 0.745). Grad-CAM consistency across
seeds fell from 0.700 to 0.235, tracking accuracy ($r=0.838$).

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions.} Record-level DP at $\varepsilon=8$ removed measurable membership leakage from
audited federated models at a balanced-accuracy cost of 0.076 (0.130 centrally), whereas per-patient guarantees cost
far more. Costs in real consortia remain untested, since clients were simulated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Artificial intelligence is changing how brain tumours are detected and graded on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI),
and its clinical value depends on training data drawn from many hospitals.\cite{ahmed2026}\footnote{Abbreviations.
AUC, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. CI, confidence interval. DP, differential privacy. DP-SGD, differentially
private stochastic gradient descent. FedAvg, federated averaging. FPR, false-positive rate. Grad-CAM,
gradient-weighted class activation mapping. IG, Integrated Gradients. IoU, intersection over union. LiRA,
likelihood-ratio attack. NCC, normalised cross-correlation. ROC, receiver operating characteristic. TPR, true-positive rate.} Federated learning lets
those hospitals train one shared model without moving scans, and early brain tumour studies showed that
federated models approach the accuracy of centrally trained ones.\cite{sheller2019,sheller2020,pati2022}

Sharing model updates is not the same as protecting patients, because updates and released models can reveal
whether a person contributed data.\cite{pati2024,knolle2026} Differential privacy (DP) bounds that risk formally,
and its practical form for deep networks, differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD), clips each
example's gradient and adds calibrated Gaussian noise.\cite{abadi2016} Slice-based MRI complicates the guarantee,
since one Figshare patient contributes a median of 13 slices and a guarantee per image covers that patient only
through group privacy.

Two further problems weaken the published evidence for private federated brain tumour classifiers. The public
four-class collections assembled from Figshare, SARTAJ and Br35H overlap, so test images can have near-copies in
training.\cite{vangala2026} The privacy mechanism, its budget and the protected unit are also often not reported in a
form that can be checked.\cite{lakshmi2026,qazi2026,gupta2026} [CHECK: the statement for lakshmi2026 and
qazi2026 rests on their abstracts, confirm against the full texts]

The present paper therefore measures, on a deduplicated benchmark with expert tumour masks, what record-level and
patient-level DP cost in accuracy, whether measured leakage follows the formal guarantee, and how explanations
change under privacy. The study uses only public data, simulated federated clients and post-hoc audits, and it
reports every result on a test set cleaned of mirrored duplicates.

\subsection{Related work}
Federated learning for brain tumours began with multi-institutional segmentation, where models trained without
sharing data matched those trained on pooled data.\cite{sheller2019,sheller2020,pati2022} Later classification
studies on the merged Figshare, SARTAJ and Br35H collections report accuracies from 94\% to 99.29\% with federated
averaging (FedAvg),\cite{mcmahan2017} FedProx, SCAFFOLD or transfer learning, and some add Grad-CAM pictures.\cite{albalawi2024,mastoi2025,muntaqim2025,paul2026}
The present work differs by training on a deduplicated, patient-disjoint benchmark and by attaching an accounted
privacy guarantee to every private model.

Differential privacy has been applied to medical imaging with dedicated DP-SGD libraries and accounting
tools.\cite{ziller2021,mironov2017,yousefpour2021} Kaissis et al.\ trained a three-site federated chest radiograph
model at $\varepsilon=6$ whose accuracy stayed comparable to that of two radiologists.\cite{kaissis2021} Adnan et
al.\ studied differentially private federated learning on histopathology with varying numbers of
providers,\cite{adnan2022} and Tayebi Arasteh et al.\ found that private training lowered accuracy mainly for
difficult diagnoses.\cite{tayebi2024} Ziller et al.\ showed that large budgets can already block reconstruction
attacks.\cite{ziller2024} These studies protect single records, whereas the present work compares record-level and
patient-level guarantees on the same data and audits both empirically.

Membership inference measures privacy empirically, and the likelihood-ratio attack (LiRA) compares a target model's
confidence on an example with that of shadow models trained with and without it.\cite{carlini2022} Knolle et al.\
reported that patients with several similar records can face
near-perfect attack success even when aggregate success looks random.\cite{knolle2026} The closest federated brain
tumour study adds Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.5 to client weights without clipping, so its stated
$\varepsilon$ of about 3.4 cannot be derived from the mechanism.\cite{gupta2026} The present work runs LiRA at the
image and the patient level against formally private models and against that unclipped mechanism.

Private training also changes explanations, since Saifullah et al.\ and Manna and Sett reported that DP alters
attributions across many datasets and methods,\cite{saifullah2024,manna2024} and Adebayo et al.\ showed that some saliency methods
ignore the learned weights.\cite{adebayo2018} The present work measures Grad-CAM localisation against expert tumour
masks, its consistency across seeds and a randomisation check, and asks whether privacy affects explanations beyond
its effect on accuracy.

Benchmark contamination is the last theme, and Vangala et al.\ found that 28.8\% of a widely used Kaggle test split
has a near-twin in training,\cite{vangala2026} and the source collections differ in class
composition.\cite{chengdata,sartajdata,br35hdata} The present work adds a mirror-aware correlation check, because
perceptual hashes do not match copies flipped left to right. Taken together, the accuracy cost and the empirical
leakage of record-level and patient-level DP for federated brain tumour MRI on contamination-checked data, together
with their effect on explanation fidelity, have not been measured side by side. The individual methods used here are
established, so the contribution of the present work lies in applying them jointly, with accounted guarantees at two
privacy units and an attack whose members and non-members are matched in composition.

\subsection{Contributions}
The study makes five contributions, each answered by the measurements summarised in the abstract.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A deduplicated, patient-disjoint benchmark.} Perceptual-hash deduplication reduced 7828 labelled
images to 3845 images split by patient, and a mirror-aware correlation check found 66 contaminated test images,
which are excluded from every reported result.
\item \textbf{An accounted privacy and utility comparison at two privacy units.} Central and federated models were
trained under record-level and patient-level DP-SGD with R\'enyi accounting and five seeds per setting.
Record-level $\varepsilon=8$ cost 0.130 in central balanced accuracy, mostly through meningioma and no-tumour
sensitivity, whereas patient-level DP needed $\varepsilon=64$ to exceed that accuracy.
\item \textbf{The effect of client count under DP.} Splitting the same images across more clients lowered accuracy
under DP by 0.096 per unit of $\ln K$ at $\varepsilon=3$, consistent with the aggregated noise scale, and FedProx
and SCAFFOLD did not recover the loss.
\item \textbf{An empirical privacy audit.} LiRA with 16 shadow models per setting identified training images and
patients in non-private federated models, found no rate significantly above chance at 1\% FPR under record-level DP
once non-members were matched to members by source and class, and still detected patients under an unclipped
weight-noise mechanism of higher utility.
\item \textbf{Explanation fidelity under privacy.} Grad-CAM localisation and cross-seed consistency declined as
$\varepsilon$ decreased, consistency correlated with accuracy at $r=0.838$ across 31 configurations, and the noise
multiplier added no explanatory power once accuracy was known.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Material and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Problem formulation and threat model}\label{sec:threat}
Consider $K$ clients, where client $j$ holds a dataset $D_j$ of $n_j$ images grouped into $U_j$ units, and
$N=\sum_j n_j=2610$ training images form $U=\sum_j U_j=634$ units. A unit is one Figshare patient, or a group of
near-identical SARTAJ or Br35H images, because those two collections carry no patient identifiers. A server runs
$T=100$ rounds, and in each round every client runs $S=10$ local steps on a model $f_\theta$ with parameters
$\theta$.

A randomised training mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-differentially private if, for all
neighbouring datasets $D$ and $D'$ and every set of outputs $O$,
\begin{equation}
\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D)\in O] \le e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D')\in O] + \delta .
\label{eq:dp}
\end{equation}
Record-level neighbours differ in one image, and patient-level neighbours differ in one unit. Smaller $\varepsilon$
means stronger protection, and $\delta=10^{-5}$ lies below $1/N$.

Every client runs DP-SGD on its own data, so each update it sends is already a private release. The guarantee
therefore holds against an honest-but-curious server, against other clients and against anyone who sees the released
model. Client sizes $n_j$ and $U_j$ and the server-held validation split are treated as public, and no secure
aggregation is used. A unit split across $m$ clients is protected by the composition of $m$ guarantees, and the worst
case over units is reported. Client-level DP, which hides the participation of a whole hospital,\cite{mcmahan2018} was
not evaluated, because with three to 15 clients the noise needed to mask one client would dominate the aggregate.

The audit adversary has query access to the logits of the released model, knows the training recipe and the data
distribution, and can train shadow models on samples of the same population. The adversary's goal is to decide
whether a given image, or a given patient, was used in training. The formal guarantee also covers every update that
a client sends, but the empirical audit tests only the released model, and attacks that reconstruct images from
individual updates\cite{ziller2024} were not run. The study pursues three goals, namely the accuracy cost of each
guarantee, the leakage that remains measurable, and the change in explanation quality.

The code enforces clipping, noise, sampling and accounting, whereas three elements rest on assumptions. The
patient-level guarantee is exact only for Figshare, the round with the lowest validation loss is selected on the public validation
split, and the federated learning-rate search adds a privacy cost that is reported but not calibrated. The central
learning rate of 0.02 was chosen among three candidates in pilot runs on the same training data, and that selection
is not included in any reported $\varepsilon$.

\subsection{Data curation and leakage control}
Three public collections were combined, namely Figshare (3064 T1-weighted contrast-enhanced slices from 233 patients
with expert tumour masks),\cite{cheng2015,chengdata} SARTAJ (3264 images)\cite{sartajdata} and Br35H (3861
images).\cite{br35hdata} Glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumour and no-tumour labels were kept, which left 7828 labelled
images, and Algorithm~\ref{alg:curation} summarises the curation steps described next.

Each image was clipped to its 0.5th and 99.5th intensity percentiles, scaled to 8 bits, padded to a square and resized
to $128\times128$ pixels with box filtering. Deduplication used a 64-bit discrete cosine transform perceptual hash and
visited images in the order Figshare, SARTAJ and Br35H, so that the expert-annotated copy was kept. An image within
Hamming distance 4 of a kept image was removed, and a label disagreement removed both images unless the kept image
came from Figshare. Deduplication removed 3934 images and label conflicts a further 49.

Split units are Figshare patients, with 48 pairs of patients that shared a slice merged into one unit, and groups of
SARTAJ and Br35H images linked within Hamming distance 6. Units were assigned to training, validation and test splits
in the proportions 70, 10 and 20\%, stratified by dominant source and label with seed 2026. The result was 2610
training, 422 validation and 813 test images in 821 training and test units and 103 validation units, with no
Figshare patient in two splits (Table~\ref{tab:data}).

Perceptual hashes do not match mirrored copies, so every pair of curated images was compared by normalised
cross-correlation (NCC) at $64\times64$ pixels, both as stored and with one image flipped left to right. A test image
with NCC of at least 0.95 to any image in another split, a threshold fixed before the check, was marked contaminated,
and the check covered $3845\times3844/2=7{,}390{,}090$ pairs in two orientations.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Curation and leakage control}\label{alg:curation}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require labelled images from Figshare, SARTAJ and Br35H, Figshare patient identifiers
\Ensure curated images, split units, splits and the set $\mathcal{C}$ of contaminated test images
\State preprocess each image (percentile clipping, 8-bit scaling, square padding, $128\times128$ box resizing)
\State compute a 64-bit perceptual hash $h_i$ for every image
\State $\mathcal{K}\gets\emptyset$
\For{each image $i$ in source order Figshare, SARTAJ, Br35H, then file path}
  \If{no $k\in\mathcal{K}$ has Hamming distance $d_H(h_i,h_k)\le4$}
    \State add $i$ to $\mathcal{K}$
  \ElsIf{the labels of $i$ and $k$ differ and $k$ is not from Figshare}
    \State remove $k$ from $\mathcal{K}$ and discard $i$ \Comment{label conflict}
  \Else
    \State discard $i$ \Comment{duplicate, or copy of a Figshare slice}
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State form units from Figshare patients, merging patients that share a slice with $d_H\le4$
\State link SARTAJ and Br35H images with $d_H\le6$ into units
\State assign units to train, validation and test (70, 10, 20\%), stratified by source and label
\For{each pair $(i,l)$ of curated images}
  \State $r_{il}\gets\max\{\mathrm{NCC}(x_i,x_l),\,\mathrm{NCC}(x_i,\mathrm{flip}(x_l))\}$ at $64\times64$
\EndFor
\State $\mathcal{C}\gets\{i \in \text{test}: r_{il}\ge0.95 \text{ for some } l \text{ in another split}\}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Federated training with record-level and patient-level DP}
Every client draws a Poisson batch $B$ in which each image is kept independently with rate $q=0.1$. Record-level
DP-SGD\cite{abadi2016} clips each per-example gradient $g_i$ to norm $C=1$, adds Gaussian noise with noise multiplier
$\sigma$ and divides by the expected batch size $q\,n_j$, which keeps the denominator independent of the data,
\begin{equation}
\tilde{g}_j = \frac{1}{q\, n_j}\left( \sum_{i \in B} g_i \min\left(1, \frac{C}{\lVert g_i \rVert_2}\right)
+ \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I\right) \right).
\label{eq:dpsgd}
\end{equation}

Patient-level DP samples units with rate $q$ instead of images. For each sampled unit $u$ with images $I_u$, the mean
gradient $\bar{g}_u=|I_u|^{-1}\sum_{i\in I_u} g_i$ is clipped, so adding or removing a whole unit changes the sum by
at most $C$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde{g}_j = \frac{1}{q\, U_j}\left( \sum_{u \in B_U} \bar{g}_u \min\left(1, \frac{C}{\lVert \bar{g}_u \rVert_2}\right)
+ \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I\right) \right).
\label{eq:unitdp}
\end{equation}

The server averages client models with size weights, $\theta\gets\sum_j (n_j/N)\,\theta_j$. Client $j$ adds noise
with standard deviation $\sigma C/(q\,n_j)$ and receives weight $n_j/N$, so each client contributes noise
$\sigma C/(qN)$ to the average whatever its size, and $K$ independent clients give
\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{agg}} = \frac{\sigma C \sqrt{K}}{qN}.
\label{eq:aggnoise}
\end{equation}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:train} lists one complete training run.

The noise multiplier $\sigma$ was calibrated by bisection so that R\'enyi DP accounting\cite{mironov2017} in
Opacus\cite{yousefpour2021} gives the target $\varepsilon$ at $\delta=10^{-5}$ over $T\times S=1000$ steps.
Record-level targets were $\varepsilon$ of 1, 3 and 8, central patient-level targets 3, 8, 16, 32 and 64, and federated
patient-level runs used $\varepsilon=8$. A clip-only ablation with $\sigma=0$ separates the cost of clipping from that
of noise. Because federated learning rates were chosen among $k=2$ candidates trained on the same records, the
$\varepsilon$ composed over both candidates is also reported.\cite{papernot2022}

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Federated training with client-side DP-SGD}\label{alg:train}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require client data $D_j$ (with units at patient level), rounds $T$, local steps $S$, rate $q$, clip norm $C$,
noise multiplier $\sigma$, learning rate $\eta_0$, momentum $\beta$, optional weight noise $s$
\Ensure released parameters $\theta^\star$ and accounted $\varepsilon$
\State initialise $\theta$ from ImageNet weights, $\theta^\star\gets\theta$
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
  \State $\eta_t\gets\eta_0\,\tfrac12\{1+\cos(\pi (t-1)/T)\}$ \Comment{cosine schedule}
  \For{each client $j$}
    \State $\theta_j\gets\theta$
    \For{$l=1,\dots,S$}
      \State draw a Poisson batch of images (record level) or units (patient level) with rate $q$
      \State compute $\tilde g_j$ by Eq.~(\ref{eq:dpsgd}) or Eq.~(\ref{eq:unitdp}) on randomly flipped images
      \State add the FedProx or SCAFFOLD correction if used, then take a momentum step with $\eta_t$ and $\beta$
    \EndFor
    \State if $s>0$, $\theta_j\gets\theta_j+\mathcal{N}(0,s^2I)$ \Comment{weight-noise comparator, no DP}
  \EndFor
  \State $\theta\gets\sum_j (n_j/N)\,\theta_j$
  \State if the validation loss of $\theta$ is the lowest so far, $\theta^\star\gets\theta$
\EndFor
\State $\varepsilon\gets$ R\'enyi accountant$(\sigma,q,TS,\delta)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Per-example gradients were computed in physical chunks of 32 examples, so memory grows with the chunk size times the
number of parameters $P$, and one local step costs $O(|B|\,P)$. Central training is the one-client case on the pooled
training set.

\subsection{Drift correction and the weight-noise comparator}
FedProx\cite{li2020} adds a proximal term with weight $\mu$ to each local objective, and $\mu$ of 0.01, 0.1, 1 and 10
was screened on validation loss at seed 0. SCAFFOLD\cite{karimireddy2020} uses option II control variates with two
corrections that the present setting requires. The control-variate normaliser uses the displacement of heavy-ball
momentum, $\eta\,M(L,\beta)$ with $M=\sum_{l=1}^{L}(1-\beta^l)/(1-\beta)$ over $L$ local steps, in place of $L\eta$,
and the server control variate is averaged with the same size weights as the model. An ablation keeps the uniform
average of the original algorithm.

To test the mechanism of the closest prior work,\cite{gupta2026} Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0,s^2)$ was added to
each client's weights after local training, without clipping or accounting. The published value $s=0.5$ was kept, and
a utility-matched $s$ was chosen by a rule fixed beforehand. The rule took the largest value in $\{0.5, 0.05, 0.01,
0.003, 0.001\}$ whose seed-0 validation loss did not exceed that of record-level $\varepsilon=8$ FedAvg on the
natural partition, which selected $s=0.003$.

\subsection{Membership inference audit}
LiRA\cite{carlini2022} was run against FedAvg on the natural partition without DP and at $\varepsilon$ of 8, 3 and 1,
and against the weight-noise comparator (Algorithm~\ref{alg:lira}). Each target configuration has 16 shadow models
trained with the target's settings on a random half of the training and test units. Every patient is therefore
wholly in or out of a shadow's training set.

The attack statistic is the logit-scaled confidence $\phi=z_y-\log\sum_{c\ne y}e^{z_c}$ of the true class $y$, where
$z$ are the logits, computed for the image ($a=1$) and its mirror image ($a=2$). For each example, Gaussian fits to the
statistics of shadows that included (IN) and excluded (OUT) it give the online score
\begin{equation}
\Lambda=\sum_{a=1}^{2}\left[\log\mathcal{N}\!\left(\phi_a;\mu^{\mathrm{in}}_a,\sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_a\right)
-\log\mathcal{N}\!\left(\phi_a;\mu^{\mathrm{out}}_a,\sigma^{\mathrm{out}}_a\right)\right],
\label{eq:lira}
\end{equation}
with per-example means and one pooled standard deviation per side, the variant suited to few shadows. A patient's
score is the sum of $\Lambda$ over the patient's slices.

Members are the training images and non-members the clean test images. Patient scores compare 126 Figshare training
units with 40 test units. True-positive rates (TPR) at 1\% and 0.1\% false-positive rate (FPR) are the primary
measures, and the area under the curve (AUC) is reported alongside. Because the clean non-members differ from the
members in source and class mix, the TPR at 1\% FPR is also computed with non-members reweighted to the members'
distribution over source and class strata, and with both groups restricted to Figshare. The diverged weight-noise
models, which classify at chance, give an empirical reference for the rate reached without a usable classifier. A loss-threshold attack, computed overall and within
each source and class stratum, served as a simpler reference.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Online LiRA at image and patient level}\label{alg:lira}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require target models, 16 shadow models with membership masks, labels $y$, Figshare units
\Ensure image-level and patient-level TPR at fixed FPR, and AUC
\For{each shadow model $m$ and each example $i$}
  \State $\phi_{m,i,a}\gets z_y-\log\sum_{c\ne y}e^{z_c}$ for the image ($a=1$) and its mirror ($a=2$)
\EndFor
\State fit $\mu^{\mathrm{in}}_{i,a}$ and $\mu^{\mathrm{out}}_{i,a}$ per example and pooled $\sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_a$ and $\sigma^{\mathrm{out}}_a$
\For{each target model}
  \State score every example by Eq.~(\ref{eq:lira}), keeping examples with at least one IN and one OUT shadow
  \State image level, training images against clean test images
  \State patient level, summed scores of Figshare training units against test units
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The audit costs 16 shadow trainings per target configuration, in addition to inference of every model on all 3845
images and their mirrors.

\subsection{Explanation evaluation}
Grad-CAM\cite{selvaraju2017} on the last residual block, Grad-CAM++\cite{chattopadhay2018} and Integrated Gradients
(IG)\cite{sundararajan2017} with a black baseline and 32 steps were computed for the true class of the 645 Figshare
test tumour images that have expert masks. At $128\times128$ input the last block has $4\times4$ resolution, and maps
are upsampled bilinearly. Localisation used the pointing game with a 3-pixel tolerance, the intersection over union
(IoU) of the top 15\% of pixels with the mask and the share of saliency inside the mask, on correctly classified
images.

Faithfulness used deletion and insertion curves over 20 steps.\cite{petsiuk2018} A model-parameter randomisation
test\cite{adebayo2018} on 64 images compared maps of each trained model with those of a fully re-initialised network.
The share of Grad-CAM mass on the outer 8-pixel rim of the brain tested for skull-border artefacts. Consistency
across seeds adapts the metric of Gupta et al.,\cite{gupta2026} with the seeds of one configuration in place of
clients, five for central and three for federated configurations. It is the IoU between the top 10\% of pixels of each
model's $8\times8$ Grad-CAM map and those of the mean map over seeds.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
Table~\ref{tab:data} gives the data per source. The natural partition assigns each source to one client, giving
clients of 2029 (Figshare), 351 (SARTAJ) and 230 (Br35H) training images with strong label skew, since no-tumour
images come only from SARTAJ and Br35H. Synthetic partitions split the same 2610 images into 10 clients uniformly at
random or by a Dirichlet prior over class proportions with $\alpha$ of 0.1, 0.5 or 5.0.\cite{hsu2019} The
$\alpha=0.5$ partition was also built with $K$ of 3, 5 and 15 clients, and a Dirichlet partition over whole units
($\alpha=0.5$, $K=10$) serves patient-level DP.

into one input channel. Batch normalisation layers stay in inference mode with their pretrained statistics, so the

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
Every setting was trained with seeds 1 to 5, and seed 0 was reserved for learning-rate and $\mu$ selection. Results
are seed means with $t$-based 95\% confidence intervals from the sample standard deviation, and SCAFFOLD was compared
with FedAvg by paired $t$-tests over seeds with Holm correction across 15 configurations. The client-count effect is
the least-squares slope of balanced accuracy on $\ln K$ over 20 runs per setting.

Explanation metrics within one model carry 95\% intervals from 2000 bootstrap resamples of test patients. The
relation between explanation quality and privacy was tested with the Pearson correlation between each metric and clean
balanced accuracy, and with an ordinary least-squares fit on accuracy and $\sigma$. Because consistency is measured
against the mean map of each configuration, its seeds are not independent, so the primary analysis uses one point per
configuration and the per-model analysis is reported alongside. Membership rates were compared with the nominal level
by one-sample $t$-tests over the five targets. Only the SCAFFOLD comparison is corrected for multiplicity, and the
other intervals and $p$-values are descriptive. Metrics are reported to three decimal places and $p$-values to three
decimal places or as $p<0.001$. These methods produced the measurements
reported next, starting with the benchmark itself.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Curated benchmark}
Table~\ref{tab:data} shows that deduplication removed more than half of the labelled images, mostly because SARTAJ and
Br35H repeat each other and Figshare. The NCC check then found 148 cross-split pairs at NCC of 0.95 or more, of which
120 matched only after flipping, and these pairs marked 66 of the 813 test images as contaminated. Of the 66, 60 were
Br35H no-tumour images, 5 SARTAJ no-tumour images and 1 a Figshare glioma.

Removing the contaminated images changed configuration means of balanced accuracy by $-0.049$ to $+0.007$, and single
runs by at most 0.072 in absolute value. Private configurations lost more on average ($-0.031$) than non-private ones
($-0.017$), so the full test set flattered private models slightly more. Every comparison below uses the 747 clean test
images, and the full-set values appear in Supplementary Table S3.

The patient-level curve recovered only at large budgets, with 0.565 at $\varepsilon=16$, 0.727 at $\varepsilon=32$ and

Record-level $\varepsilon=8$ gives little formal protection per patient. A Figshare patient contributes a median of
13 slices, and group privacy turns an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ guarantee for one image into
$(k\varepsilon,\,k e^{(k-1)\varepsilon}\delta)$ for $k$ images.\cite{dwork2014} For $k=13$, $\varepsilon=8$ and
$\delta=10^{-5}$ the second term is $13e^{96}\times10^{-5}\approx6\times10^{37}$, far above 1, so the record-level
guarantee bounds nothing for such a patient. Composing over the two
learning-rate candidates raised federated record-level $\varepsilon=8$ to 11.98 (Supplementary Table S7).

\subsection{Client count under DP}
Fig.~\ref{fig:clients} shows that, with the same 2610 images split across more clients, accuracy fell under DP but not
without it. At $\varepsilon=3$ the slope was $-0.096$ per unit of $\ln K$ ($p<0.001$), from 0.677 with three clients
to 0.525 with 15. At $\varepsilon=8$ the slope was $-0.156$ ($p<0.001$) with learning rate 0.01, from 0.779 to 0.525,
and $-0.016$ ($p=0.380$) with the rate of 0.003 selected for 10 clients (dashed in Fig.~\ref{fig:clients}),
whereas without DP the slope was
$+0.013$ ($p=0.079$).

\subsection{Drift correction}
Neither FedProx nor SCAFFOLD recovered the utility lost to DP. FedProx with $\mu$ of 0.01 or 0.1 changed the seed-0
validation loss of FedAvg by at most 0.0098 in all 15 configurations, and $\mu=1$ changed it by $-0.013$ to
$+0.038$. A weight of $\mu=10$ raised the loss in every configuration without DP and at $\varepsilon=8$
(Supplementary Table S10).

Size-weighted SCAFFOLD did not differ from FedAvg in any configuration after Holm correction, with the smallest adjusted
$p=0.064$. With the uniform server average of the original algorithm, SCAFFOLD collapsed on unequal clients, reaching
0.590 against 0.825 for size-weighted SCAFFOLD on the natural partition at $\varepsilon=8$ (full test set,
Supplementary Table S11).

\subsection{Explanations}
Grad-CAM localisation against expert masks declined as $\varepsilon$ decreased. For correctly classified test images of
central models, the pointing-game hit rate fell from 0.197 without DP to 0.156 at $\varepsilon=8$ and 0.089 at
$\varepsilon=1$, and the top-15\% IoU fell from 0.087 to 0.032. Masks cover 1.76\% of the image on average (0.01758).
On the same images, the share of Grad-CAM mass inside the mask was 0.0323 against a mask area of 0.0177 without DP,
1.82 times the share of a uniform map, and 0.0267 against 0.0201 at $\varepsilon=1$, 1.33 times. Localisation therefore
stayed above chance but far from precise.

Fig.~\ref{fig:xai}a shows that consistency across seeds fell from 0.700 without DP to 0.663 with clipping alone and to
0.485, 0.309 and 0.235 at $\varepsilon$ of 8, 3 and 1. Patient-level models stayed between 0.218 and 0.291 up to
$\varepsilon=32$ and reached 0.368 at $\varepsilon=64$. Some of these models classify near chance, such as patient-level
$\varepsilon=3$ at 0.251, and their consistency describes maps of a model that has learned little.

\subsection{Source controls}
The source collection was linearly decodable from penultimate features in every setting. Among tumour images, a probe
separated Figshare from SARTAJ with balanced accuracy 0.913 for the central model without DP and 0.851 at
$\varepsilon=1$. The ImageNet initialisation reached 0.871 and a random network 0.846, so training added only 0.042
($0.913-0.871$). Accuracy on SARTAJ alone, the one source with all four classes, was 0.663 for central models
without DP on the full test set, against 0.919 pooled (Supplementary Table S5). These measurements raise the question
of what the privacy budget actually buys, which Section~\ref{sec:discussion} addresses.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Protection bought by the privacy budget}
The audit and the accounting agree for the federated models that were attacked. Without DP, a federated model trained
on 2610 images leaked enough for LiRA to flag one training image in ten at 1\% FPR, and aggregating over slices made
the patient-level signal stronger still (AUC 0.805). Record-level DP at $\varepsilon=8$ left no rate at 1\% FPR
significantly above chance once members and non-members were matched in composition, at a cost of 0.076 in balanced
accuracy. The unmatched rate of 0.017 suggests that a composition gap between members and non-members can mimic leakage,
so audits on merged collections should match the two groups. Central and patient-level models were not attacked, and
an attack that fails does not prove that no stronger attack exists, although the result agrees with reports that
moderate budgets defeat practical attacks.\cite{ziller2024} Counting the learning-rate search, the federated
guarantee is $\varepsilon=11.98$ instead of 8.

The weight-noise comparator separates noise from guarantee, since unclipped noise on weights can be tuned to keep
accuracy, as at $s=0.003$, but it then leaves training patients identifiable (AUC 0.745), and at the published $s=0.5$ it
destroyed the model in this reproduction. Details such as model size and normalisation may differ from the original
implementation, but neither setting supports a stated $\varepsilon$ for that mechanism.\cite{gupta2026}

\subsection{Cost of patient-level protection}
Patient-level DP protects the unit that matters clinically but needed a budget eight times larger for comparable
accuracy. The clip-only control (0.872) shows that weighting each patient once is harmless, so the loss comes from
noise divided among 634 units instead of 2610 images. The finding supports the observation of Knolle et al.\ that
patients with several records carry the real privacy risk,\cite{knolle2026} and suggests that a larger number of
patients is the most direct route to useful per-patient guarantees.

\subsection{Client count and drift correction}
Under DP, accuracy fell as the same data were spread across more clients, while label skew changed accuracy at $K=10$
and $\varepsilon=3$ by at most 0.010 ($0.593-0.583$). The trend is consistent with Eq.~(\ref{eq:aggnoise}), because
each client adds independent noise without adding data. The arithmetic is standard for DP-SGD and is not claimed as new. It
implies that consortia of few large sites lose least, and that a learning rate tuned for one client count can hide
the effect at another.

Drift correction likely did not help because one local epoch per round produced little drift, while noise dominated the
error. The SCAFFOLD ablation also indicates that control variates must be averaged with the same weights as the model,
or the correction harms the unequal and noisy settings in which it is most needed.

\subsection{Explanations under privacy}
DP degraded Grad-CAM localisation and cross-seed consistency, in line with reports on attribution methods in
general.\cite{saifullah2024,manna2024} For brain tumour MRI, the degradation followed accuracy ($r=0.838$ for
consistency and $r=0.903$ for IoU across configurations), and $\sigma$ added nothing once accuracy was known. The
small change under clipping alone (0.700 to 0.663) suggests that noise affects explanations mainly through the
accuracy it costs, although the correlational design cannot establish that pathway.

The low absolute localisation, with a pointing hit rate of 0.197 even without DP, is consistent with the $4\times4$
resolution of the last ResNet block at 128-pixel input. Clinically, DP cost most in meningioma and no-tumour
sensitivity while missed tumours stayed rare (Table~\ref{tab:class}), so a private model would mainly need review of
its tumour-type calls. IG with a black baseline failed the randomisation test, which argues against
reporting such maps without that check.\cite{adebayo2018}

\subsection{Benchmark leakage and source confounding}
The correlation check found mirrored duplicates that perceptual hashing missed, concentrated in the Br35H no-tumour
class, and training with random flips can then show a model the exact test image. Source was also decodable from images
of one class even by a random network, so headline accuracy on merged collections partly measures which collection an
image came from, as Vangala et al.\ reported for centralised benchmarks.\cite{vangala2026} Accuracies reported on these
collections\cite{albalawi2024,mastoi2025,muntaqim2025,paul2026} should therefore not be read as clinical performance,
and flip-invariant deduplication with per-source reporting should become routine.

\subsection{Limitations}
Mirrored pairs remain between training and validation data (47 pairs), so round selection used slightly contaminated
validation data, although every test result excludes contaminated images. SARTAJ and Br35H carry no patient
identifiers, so their units are groups of similar images and the patient-level guarantee is exact only for Figshare.

Shadow models trained on about 1700 images against 2610 for the targets, which shifts average-case AUC, so the
conclusions rest on low-FPR rates. Only 40 Figshare test units were available, so patient-level TPR below 2.5\% FPR
cannot be resolved. The membership audit covered only FedAvg on the natural partition at record level, so the leakage
of central and patient-level models and of the update channel was not measured. All models are two-dimensional
ResNet-18 classifiers on axial, coronal and sagittal slices from three public sources, and the federated partitions
were simulated, not drawn from real institutions. Learning rates were chosen on private validation data, the
$\varepsilon$ composed over both federated candidates is reported but was not used for calibration, and the central
learning-rate selection is not accounted. Federated explanation metrics rest on three seeds per configuration.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
On a deduplicated three-source brain MRI benchmark, a likelihood-ratio membership attack exposed non-private
federated models but found no image-level rate significantly above chance under record-level DP at $\varepsilon=8$,
once members and non-members were matched in source and class, and its patient-level AUC was 0.454. The price was a
balanced-accuracy loss of 0.076 for federated and 0.130 for central training, borne mainly by meningioma and no-tumour
sensitivity. A guarantee per patient cost far
more and needed $\varepsilon=64$, because the noise is shared among 634 patients and patient groups instead of 2610
slices.

Under DP, splitting fixed data across more clients lowered accuracy through the aggregated noise scale, and drift
correction did not offset the loss. Unclipped weight noise either destroyed the model or left patients identifiable, so
it should not be reported with an $\varepsilon$. Grad-CAM localisation and consistency degraded with privacy in step
with accuracy, and a composition gap between members and non-members was enough to push the attack rate on a
private model significantly above chance.

Future work should test these costs on real multi-site data with patient identifiers, extend the audit to
three-dimensional models and segmentation, attack central, patient-level and update-level releases, and study larger
patient cohorts in which per-patient guarantees become affordable. Federated studies on public brain tumour collections should use flip-invariant deduplication, state
$\varepsilon$, $\delta$ and the privacy unit, and test their privacy claims with an attack.
